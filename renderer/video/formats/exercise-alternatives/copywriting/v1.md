# Copywriting: exercise-alternatives v1

## 1. Scope and evidence

This file owns how a selected LIFT CODE message is expressed through language in the `exercise-alternatives` video format: constraint framing, paired exercise labels, wording, rhythm, spoken-text use, CTA, title, and caption.

- Message meaning and claim limits come from the selected message version.
- Product truth comes from `context/product.md`.
- Reusable strength-training knowledge, provenance, evidence status, scope, and exceptions come from `context/expertise.md` and its linked topic owners.
- Each native video project owns exact canvas, geometry, typography, clip timing, transitions, crop, production assets, muscle overlays, and audio levels.
- `context/imagery.md` owns the account-wide art direction and image-generation or selection policy.
- `renderer/video/formats/exercise-alternatives/references/post-01/workout_inspiration1.mov` is one user-designated source video.
- The reference is a silent 27.263-second video with seven sequential paired exercise demonstrations. Each comparison remains visible for roughly one complete repetition before changing.
- The reference labels the demonstrations only as `Gym` and `Home`, uses anatomical target overlays, and does not show exercise names, selection rationale, set-and-rep prescriptions, or equivalence evidence.
- A 4.517-second source-app demonstration interrupts the exercise sequence. That product insert is not part of the reusable comparison grammar and must not be adapted during the current no-product prelaunch phase.
- The captured search bar, close button, navigation controls, mute control, and black surround are source-viewer chrome, not format grammar.
- References establish execution and language technique only. Never copy their exercise pairings, anatomical assets, source interface, product screen, wording, or branding.

This format helps a lifter choose a practical exercise option when the intended exercise, machine, environment, or equipment is unavailable. It does not own exercise equivalence, target-muscle facts, biomechanics, programming, or safety guidance. Before drafting, each proposed pairing must be supported by bounded admitted expertise with a clear rationale and relevant limitations.

## 2. Eligible situations

Choose exactly one constraint for one content. Do not mix unrelated reasons for substitution merely to increase the number of demonstrations.

### Intended machine or station is unavailable

Use when the lifter is at the Gym but the planned machine, rack, cable station, or other equipment is occupied or unavailable.

```text
[planned exercise or unavailable equipment]
[available alternative]
```

- Frame the alternative as a way to continue the intended training goal without pretending that the exercises are identical.
- The alternative must use equipment that is plausibly available in the stated situation.
- Do not instruct the viewer to improvise an unsafe setup, interfere with another lifter, or use equipment outside its intended function.

### The lifter cannot reach the Gym

Use when the training environment changes and the viewer has a stated set of available equipment.

```text
[Gym exercise or planned movement]
[available environment and equipment]
[practical alternative]
```

- State the actual equipment constraint: `Dumbbells`, `Bands`, `Bodyweight`, or another verified option.
- Do not use `Home` as if it automatically means no equipment.
- Keep all alternatives inside the same declared equipment condition unless the opening explicitly presents several distinct options.

### Another equipment constraint

A travel setup, broken machine, crowded facility, or limited hotel Gym may qualify when the constraint is specific and the resulting alternatives are useful.

- Name the constraint rather than relying on a vague `no equipment` or `limited setup` claim.
- Select another format when the useful answer requires extensive form coaching, programming changes, pain guidance, or rehabilitation advice.

## 3. Copy flow

### Opening channel

Choose one of two approaches according to the approved message and how much context the paired labels can carry.

1. **Constraint hook:** state the unavailable option and what the viewer can still accomplish.
2. **Label-only opening:** begin directly with the first pair when the environment and available equipment are immediately clear.

A constraint hook may use this structure:

```text
[planned option unavailable?]
[bounded promise to show practical alternatives]
```

- Make the relevant constraint legible before the first alternative is judged.
- Promise options, not identical results or guaranteed performance.
- Do not manufacture urgency, shame the viewer for changing an exercise, or frame adaptation as weakness.
- Keep the full hook short enough that it does not delay the first useful pair.

### Paired exercise labels

Every comparison must make the situation-to-alternative relationship immediately legible. Labels that only name or classify the two exercises are not sufficient because they do not explain why the alternative matters.

Possible label roles are:

```text
Planned
[exercise name]

Available now
[alternative name]
```

```text
Gym
[exercise name]

Home—[equipment]
[alternative name]
```

```text
No [unavailable equipment]
Try [alternative name]
```

```text
Machine taken?
Do this with [available equipment] instead
```

- Lead with the practical relationship: unavailable context above, usable alternative below. This is the value carried by the reference's `Gym` and `Home` labels; adapt the relationship rather than copying those words when the actual constraint differs.
- Include the precise, idiomatic U.S.-English exercise names when they fit without displacing the situation and value. When the animation makes the pair unambiguous and names would weaken immediate comprehension, the on-screen labels may carry only the situation-to-alternative relationship; keep exact identity available in the approved caption.
- Do not require the viewer to reverse-engineer an exact variation when that identity is material to truthful or safe use.
- Keep label grammar parallel across all pairs in one video.
- Use the same situation labels throughout one content unless the approved opening explicitly defines a comparison matrix.
- State required equipment when it is not obvious.
- A target-area label or muscle overlay may be used only when its meaning and scope are supported. Matching highlights do not prove matching stimulus.
- Default to no set-and-rep prescription. Add one only when the exact prescription is supported for the selected situation and does not imply that the alternative should inherit the original exercise's loading.
- Do not use `exact replacement`, `same results`, `identical stimulus`, `just as effective`, or another unsupported equivalence claim.
- Prefer bounded language such as `alternative`, `option`, or `try this when...`.

### Sequence and rhythm

The reference supports a sequence of short, immediately readable comparisons rather than one dense explanation.

- Match singular and plural promises exactly: an opening that promises `options`, a list, or a routine must deliver several defensible pairs rather than one demonstration.
- The default expression of this format is progression across multiple pairs. Use a single pair only when the approved direction is explicitly one focused comparison; do not present that exception as a multi-option sequence.
- Keep every pair on screen long enough to identify both names and observe at least one useful movement cycle.
- Use only as many pairs as remain coherent under the opening promise.
- Keep each pair relevant to the same unavailable situation, training goal, routine, or body-area scope.
- Do not insert a product screen, separate educational detour, or unrelated exercise merely to extend runtime.
- Exact pair duration, clip phase, transition, and total runtime belong to the native project rather than this copywriting version.

### Spoken text

The reference contains no audio. Default `spoken_text` to an empty channel.

Use spoken text only when a necessary limitation cannot remain intelligible through the opening, pair labels, and caption. Spoken text must add a decision-relevant distinction rather than narrate visible movement.

## 4. Claim and decision boundaries

An alternative may preserve part of the intended training purpose without reproducing every property of the original exercise.

- Do not infer interchangeability from a shared muscle highlight, similar body position, or visual resemblance.
- Trace the practical pairing rationale to admitted expertise before using it.
- Preserve material differences in joint action, range of motion, resistance profile, stability demand, available loading, progression, or setup when those differences affect the recommendation.
- Distinguish an equipment-constrained option from a universal exercise ranking.
- Do not imply that changing an exercise preserves the same load, repetition target, effort, or progression step automatically.
- Do not use this format for pain, injury, medical limitation, or rehabilitation substitutions.
- If no defensible alternative fits the stated constraint, reject the pairing rather than filling the sequence with a weaker match.

## 5. Writing

### Voice and rhythm

- Use idiomatic U.S. English.
- Sound practical, controlled, precise, and physically grounded rather than apologetic, clinical, or gym-bro theatrical.
- Treat adaptation as disciplined training judgment, not a compromise in seriousness.
- Prefer concrete words such as machine, cable, dumbbell, band, bench, row, press, raise, curl, available, and occupied.
- Avoid `hack`, `secret`, `best substitute`, `perfect replacement`, guaranteed-result language, and unsupported `science says` authority.
- Do not use the word `CODE` as a hacker or software metaphor.
- Run a separate exercise-name and idiomatic-English pass before showing final copy.

### Information limit

This format is for immediate exercise recognition and equipment-constrained choice, not layered explanation.

- One short constraint is the maximum sentence-level argument.
- The remaining language identifies the intended exercise, available equipment, and practical alternative.
- Keep form coaching, mechanism explanation, programming caveats, and extensive differences out of the paired label system.
- Select another format when the audience must understand why the pairing works or how to modify execution in detail.

## 6. CTA, title, and caption

The reusable reference grammar has no dedicated CTA. Default to no on-screen CTA so every segment delivers an exercise option.

- When appropriate, place one relevant action in the caption, such as saving the alternatives for a crowded Gym day.
- Provide a separate concise post title with every final copy. The title is not part of the caption.
- Compose the caption with one concise description, one important equipment or non-equivalence boundary when needed, and a small set of relevant hashtags.
- Let the caption state the situation or limitation that cannot fit naturally in the paired labels.
- Do not use the caption to repair an unsupported pairing after publication.
- Do not introduce product promotion during the current no-product prelaunch phase.
