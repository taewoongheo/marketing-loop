# Copywriting: routine-sequence v1

## 1. Scope and evidence

This file owns how a selected LIFT CODE message is expressed through language in the `routine-sequence` video format: routine framing, progress labels, exercise names, prescriptions, wording, rhythm, spoken-text use, CTA, title, and caption.

- Message meaning and claim limits come from the selected message version.
- Product truth comes from `context/product.md`.
- Reusable strength-training knowledge, provenance, evidence status, scope, and exceptions come from `context/expertise.md` and its linked topic owners.
- Each native video project owns exact canvas, geometry, typography, clip order and duration, transitions, crop, production assets, progress-marker placement, muscle overlays, and audio levels.
- `context/imagery.md` owns the account-wide art direction and image-generation or selection policy.
- `renderer/video/formats/routine-sequence/references/post-01/workout_inspiration2.mov` is one user-designated source video.
- The reference is a silent 15.415-second video. Its first 8.942 seconds show five sequential dumbbell exercise demonstrations, with a persistent five-item target-muscle strip and a green check moving to the current item.
- The source demonstrations do not display exercise names, sets, repetitions, rest, routine purpose, ordering rationale, audience, or equipment context beyond the visible dumbbells and bench.
- A 5.875-second source-app insert follows the exercise sequence and opens an individual exercise library entry. It does not show a saved routine or routine execution flow. That product insert is not part of the reusable v1 grammar and must not be adapted during the current no-product prelaunch phase.
- The captured search bar, close button, navigation controls, mute control, black surround, and terminal black tail are source-viewer or capture artifacts, not format grammar.
- References establish execution and language technique only. Never copy their exercise sequence, prescriptions, anatomical assets, source interface, product screen, wording, or branding.

This format presents one coherent exercise order by showing each movement at useful size while preserving visible progress through the full sequence. It does not own routine design, exercise selection, ordering, prescription, target-muscle facts, biomechanics, or safety guidance. Research and admit the complete bounded routine knowledge before drafting rather than treating a visually coherent list as a validated routine.

## 2. Content eligibility

### Complete routine

Call a content a `routine` only when the approved knowledge supports all generation-affecting components required for the intended use:

- a specific training goal or scope;
- a defined audience or relevant experience boundary when needed;
- available equipment and environment;
- an intentional exercise selection and order;
- a supported set-and-rep, duration, or other completion prescription;
- a relevant rest rule or a deliberate reason to omit one;
- material limitations needed to prevent overgeneralization.

Do not imply that five exercises, a muscle-map strip, or an orderly visual sequence is sufficient evidence of a complete routine.

### Exercise sequence

When the evidence supports useful exercise ideas but not a complete prescription, label the content as an `exercise sequence`, `exercise options`, or another accurate bounded description.

- Do not use `routine`, `workout`, `complete`, or `full` merely because several exercises appear in order.
- Do not invent sets, reps, rest, or ordering logic to make the content appear more actionable.
- Select another format when the viewer must understand extensive programming rationale, form coaching, pain guidance, or rehabilitation advice.

## 3. Copy flow

### Opening channel

Open with the exact scope needed to interpret the sequence.

```text
[routine or sequence goal]
[available equipment or environment]
[optional audience or duration boundary]
```

- State whether the viewer is seeing a routine or a looser exercise sequence.
- Make the main training scope understandable before the second exercise appears.
- Include the equipment condition when it materially limits who can perform the sequence.
- Use a duration claim only when the complete timing, sets, reps, rest, and transitions support it.
- Do not promise guaranteed strength, hypertrophy, calorie burn, pain relief, or completion in a fixed time without the required evidence.
- Keep the opening short enough that the first useful demonstration is not delayed.

A label-only opening is valid when the routine title, equipment, and first exercise label already provide sufficient context.

### Progress channel

Maintain a compact representation of the sequence while one exercise is demonstrated.

Possible progress language is:

```text
1 / [total]
```

```text
Step 1 of [total]
```

```text
[current exercise name]
[completed or upcoming sequence labels]
```

- The progress channel communicates order and current position, not exercise quality, scientific certainty, or completion of real training.
- Keep total count stable throughout the video.
- Use one current-state treatment consistently. Do not make an unperformed exercise look completed.
- A target-muscle thumbnail may supplement an exercise label only when the target claim is supported and the image remains legible.
- Matching or adjacent muscle highlights do not establish routine completeness, balance, or ordering validity.
- Do not require the source reference's green check, muscle-map thumbnails, exact item count, or visual arrangement. Those are execution evidence rather than reusable fixed values.

### Exercise and prescription channel

Each exercise segment must be identifiable without requiring the viewer to infer an exact variation from animation alone.

For a complete routine:

```text
[current step / total]
[exercise name]
[sets] × [reps or rep range]
[optional side, duration, or rest instruction]
```

For a bounded exercise sequence:

```text
[current step / total]
[exercise name]
[optional target or equipment label]
```

- Use the precise, idiomatic U.S.-English exercise name supported by admitted expertise.
- Keep label grammar and prescription notation parallel across all segments.
- Use a set-and-rep or duration label only when the exact prescription is supported for the stated routine, audience, equipment, and goal.
- Do not inherit a load, repetition target, or progression step from another exercise automatically.
- State unilateral sides or alternating execution when omitting that detail would make completion ambiguous.
- Keep rest global when one rule applies to the full routine; use per-exercise rest only when the difference is necessary and supported.
- Do not compress form coaching, mechanism explanation, and programming caveats into the exercise label.

### Sequence and rhythm

The reference supports short sequential demonstrations with persistent progress rather than several simultaneous exercise tiles.

- Show each exercise long enough to read its label and observe at least one useful movement cycle.
- Keep the current demonstration visually dominant while the progress channel preserves the broader routine context.
- Order every segment exactly as the approved routine or sequence requires.
- Use only as many exercises as the opening promise and runtime can support clearly.
- Do not insert unrelated education, an unsupported exercise, or a product screen merely to extend runtime.
- Exact clip phase, segment duration, transition, and total runtime belong to the native project rather than this copywriting version.

### Spoken text

The reference contains no audio. Default `spoken_text` to an empty channel.

Use spoken text only when a necessary routine boundary cannot remain intelligible through the opening, progress, exercise labels, and caption. Spoken text must add decision-relevant context rather than narrate every visible exercise.

## 4. Routine and claim boundaries

- Trace every exercise, sequence decision, target statement, and prescription to admitted expertise before using it.
- Distinguish a useful routine for a specific scope from a universal or optimal routine.
- Do not infer balance, completeness, or effectiveness from the number of exercises or the number of highlighted muscles.
- Do not claim that a routine is suitable for `everyone`, `all levels`, or every training frequency without support.
- Do not use a single target-muscle image as evidence of primary stimulus, exercise superiority, or complete coverage.
- Do not imply that watching the sequence is equivalent to receiving personalized programming.
- Do not use this compact format for pain, injury, medical limitation, or rehabilitation programming.
- If a material limitation cannot fit in the opening, labels, or caption without weakening usability, select a format that supports explanation.

## 5. Writing

### Voice and rhythm

- Use idiomatic U.S. English.
- Sound practical, controlled, precise, and physically grounded rather than motivational, clinical, or ornamental.
- Treat order, rest, and prescription as deliberate decisions rather than decorative numbers.
- Prefer concrete words such as row, curl, press, raise, dumbbell, bench, sets, reps, rest, and control.
- Avoid `ultimate`, `perfect`, `complete` without evidence, `secret`, `hack`, guaranteed-result language, and unsupported `science says` authority.
- Do not use the word `CODE` as a hacker or software metaphor.
- Run a separate exercise-name, prescription-consistency, and idiomatic-English pass before showing final copy.

### Information limit

This format is for immediate routine recognition and ordered demonstration, not layered explanation.

- One short scope statement is the maximum sentence-level argument.
- The remaining language identifies progress, exercises, and approved prescriptions.
- Put one global rest rule or scope boundary in the opening or caption instead of repeating it in every segment.
- Select another format when the audience must understand why the routine works, how to progress it, or how to choose among several constraints.

## 6. Ending, CTA, title, and caption

During the current prelaunch phase, end on the final useful exercise rather than the source product screen.

- Default to no dedicated on-screen CTA or product reveal.
- When appropriate, place one relevant action in the caption, such as saving the routine for the stated equipment condition.
- Provide a separate concise post title with every final copy. The title is not part of the caption.
- Compose the caption with one concise description, the most important audience, equipment, rest, or scope boundary, and a small set of relevant hashtags.
- Do not use the caption to repair an unsupported routine or prescription after publication.
- Do not introduce product promotion during the current no-product prelaunch phase.
